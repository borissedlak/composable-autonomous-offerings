Pegasus · TSC · modeling assumptions

Does Pegasus need services to scale linearly with workers?

The measurements show that many services do not scale linearly: an item takes longer when its service has more workers. This page separates what the method actually assumes, shows which part the data break, and lays out the options for the paper.

Short answer. The bound itself does not need linear scaling. It takes, per node, an execution-time distribution and a number of workers c, and it is correct for whatever distribution it receives. Linear scaling enters only through the training data: the models never see c, so a benchmark with one worker stands in for every c.

The assumption the bound does rest on is that execution times do not depend on the state of the queue (Assumption 3). One of the two measured effects breaks it. That effect can still be handled with a conservative bound, at the price of tightness at low load.

Two assumptions that look like one

“The service scales linearly” bundles two different statements, and they live in different parts of the pipeline.

A1: no dependence on the worker count

The distribution of one item's execution time is the same whether the service has 1 or c workers.

Needed by: the data budget only. The bound already takes c as a parameter and accepts a different distribution per configuration.

A2: no dependence on the queue state

Execution times are independent draws from one distribution, whatever the queue looks like, in particular however many workers are busy.

Needed by: the bound. Its derivation multiplies moment-generating functions of independent items. A per-item time that depends on the queue is not covered.

What the measurements show

The unthrottled chains of the second campaign ran every quality configuration at several worker counts (1 to 4, single-threaded workers, c + 0.5 cores, no throttling). Two effects appear, and each service shows mostly one of them. The two example services below are the clearest cases.

Effect 1: slower even alone

With more than one worker, items are slower even when no other item of the service runs. For the point-cloud encoder the step appears at two workers already, and the per-item ratio next to others stays at 1.

Depends on: the configuration (the worker count), not on the load.
Breaks: A1 only.
Also seen in: image-transformer, voice-detector, pc-clusterer, pc-downsampler, image-denoiser.
Cause: unknown. Candidates are a different harness code path for one versus several workers, or two workers sharing one physical core (hyperthreading).

Effect 2: slower next to busy workers

Within one run, items are slower the more items of their own service are being processed at the same time. For the pitch tracker the penalty grows with every additional busy worker.

Depends on: how many workers are busy, which depends on the arrivals: a load effect.
Breaks: A2, and A1 with it.
Also seen in: face-detector, audio-denoiser, cv-analyzer.
Cause: contention for shared hardware (caches, memory bandwidth, hyperthreads, lower boost clock with more active cores).

Two explanations were checked and ruled out within runs: activity at other services of the chain, and the idle time of a worker before an item. About a third of the services show neither effect.

Is this known from queueing theory?

The classical models assume what Pegasus assumes. M/M/c, M/G/c, Kingman's bound and the Allen–Cunneen approximation all model c identical, independent servers whose service time does not depend on c or on how many servers are busy. Capacity is exactly c·μ in those models. The diminishing returns they describe are in the waiting time (the pooling effect), not in the service time. Pegasus is on standard ground, and the effect measured here lies outside what those models describe.

Systems performance modeling does know the effect.

  • Load-dependent service centers in mean-value analysis have a service rate that is a function of the number of jobs present (Lazowska et al., Quantitative System Performance, 1984).
  • The Universal Scalability Law (Gunther) writes the throughput of N parallel workers as X(N) = λN / (1 + σ(N − 1) + κN(N − 1)), with σ for contention and κ for coherency costs. Amdahl's law is the special case with κ = 0.
  • On multicore hosts the usual causes are shared last-level cache, memory bandwidth, hyperthread siblings and frequency scaling, which matches the pattern of Effect 2.

So the reviewer-proof framing is: Pegasus uses the classical service model as a simplification, and the correction is a known one from capacity planning.

Why Effect 2 can still be bounded

A FIFO queue with c workers follows the Kiefer–Wolfowitz recursion. Wn is the sorted vector of remaining work at the c workers when item n arrives, Sn the execution time of item n (seconds), Tn the gap to the next arrival (seconds), and e1 = (1, 0, …, 0):

Wn+1 = sort( ( Wn + Sn·e1 − Tn·1 )+ )

Every step is nondecreasing in Sn, so every item's waiting time and latency can only grow when any execution time grows. This gives a coupling argument:

  1. Let Fcmax be the execution-time distribution when all c workers are busy.
  2. If the real execution time of an item, given any number of busy workers, is stochastically no larger than Fcmax, each real time can be paired with a draw from Fcmax that is at least as long.
  3. The queue fed with those draws is an ordinary queue with i.i.d. execution times, so A2 holds for it and the bound applies. By the monotonicity of the recursion, its latencies are at least the real ones.

Condition to state: the slowdown is largest with all workers busy. The within-run ratios support this: for the affected services they increase from one to two to three busy neighbors.
Price: the bound is looser at low load, when fewer workers are busy. The tail of the latency comes mostly from periods with a long queue, when all workers are busy anyway, so the loss is concentrated in the mean.

Suggested path

  1. Measure the damage first. Split the B.1 coverage by worker count and by service, and check whether misses concentrate on the services with large ratios. This needs no retraining.
  2. In the paper: present A1 as a modeling simplification, not a requirement of the bound. Name A2 (Assumption 3) as the real premise, and cite the classical service model as its origin and load-dependent stations and the Universal Scalability Law as the known correction.
  3. In the method: add the worker count to the model inputs (O2) and use the all-busy distribution (O3). If no new benchmarks are possible, fit the contention factor on the existing campaign instead (O4), and state the monotonicity condition with the within-run evidence.
  4. To explain Effect 1: read the harness code for the one-worker path, or run one service at a fixed worker count with varying cores.
