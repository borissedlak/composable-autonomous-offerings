How much does a service slow down with more workers?

Pegasus assumes that the execution time of an item does not depend on w, the number of workers (parallel single-threaded processes) of its service, so that a service with w workers has w times the capacity of one worker. This page measures, per service, how far that holds on the testbed.

Execution time relative to one worker

Each row is a service. A dot is the factor by which an execution-time statistic is multiplied when the service runs with w workers instead of one, for the same quality configuration; the whisker is its 95% bootstrap interval. The gray line at 1.0 is the Pegasus assumption. The x-axis is logarithmic, so 1.25× and 0.8× are equally far from 1. Rows are sorted by the rightmost series.

All numbers per service

Ratios at four workers unless the column says otherwise; brackets hold the 95% interval. Click a column header to sort. “Projected capacity” is the throughput at full load relative to one worker, w / (alone ratio at w × per-item ratio next to w − 1 others); linear scaling would give exactly w. It is empty where no run had enough items next to w − 1 others (low load per worker).

What is measured, and how

Data

Three measurements per service

  • Total (mean, p95). For each run, the campaign records the mean and the 95th percentile of the execution time (finish minus start of an item at a worker, without waiting in the queue). Per service, the logarithm of the statistic is fitted with one intercept per quality configuration plus one offset per worker count; the factor for w workers is exp(offset). Matching on the configuration removes the effect of the quality knobs. This is the effect at the load at which the campaign ran: the arrival rate puts the slowest service of the chain at utilization 0.5, so most services run well below that.
  • Per concurrent item (within run). Within one run the configuration and the arrival rate are fixed. The median execution time of the items that started while k other items of the same service were in process is divided by the median of the items that started alone, for k = 1, 2, 3; the page shows the median of that ratio over runs. This is the interference between the workers of one service, independent of how often the campaign's load made them overlap. At full load every item runs next to w − 1 others.
  • Items that started alone. The median execution time of the items that started with no other item of their service in process, matched across worker counts like the total. Any slowdown here is not caused by concurrency within the service.

Explanations that were checked and ruled out

  • Other services of the chain. Among items that started alone, those that started while another service of the chain was processing an item are compared with those that started while no other service was busy (column “Other svcs”). The ratio stays close to 1 for every service.
  • Worker idle time. With more workers, each worker idles longer between its own items, and an earlier analysis (explain_6) found slowdowns after idle. Within runs, the third of the alone items with the longest idle time of their worker is compared with the third with the shortest (column “Idle”). The ratio stays small for every service and, for the services with a large alone slowdown, far below that slowdown (compare the columns “Idle” and “Alone”).
  • CPU throttling. No run of this folder was throttled: every service had w + 0.5 cores, and every worker runs one thread.

What remains open

For several services (most clearly the point-cloud encoder and the image transformer) items are slower at w > 1 even when they start alone, and the slowdown appears already at two workers rather than growing with w. That pattern fits a difference in how the service harness runs one worker versus several (for example in-process versus a process pool that copies the payload), or a placement effect: the containers may use both hyperthreads of a physical core (cpuset 2-15,18-31 on a 16-core / 32-thread host), and fewer cores per worker (1.5 at one worker, 1.125 at four) makes sharing a core more likely. The campaign data cannot separate these; the harness code or a run that varies cores at a fixed worker count can.

Assumptions

  • The worker effect is multiplicative and the same for every quality configuration of a service (log-scale fit).
  • Configurations are independent units for the bootstrap; runs of one configuration are resampled together, because they share whatever the knobs do not describe. Within-run ratios treat runs as independent.
  • The projected capacity multiplies the alone ratio and the per-item ratio, and assumes that the per-item ratio measured at the campaign's loads also holds when all workers are busy.

What it means for Pegasus

The models are trained on benchmarks with one worker, and the bounds assume an execution time that does not depend on w. For the services with a ratio clearly above 1 the bounds use execution times that are too small at w > 1, by the factor in the chart; the tail (p95) is affected more than the mean for most of them. Two remedies follow from the decomposition: give the model the number of workers as an input (covers the alone part), and let the execution time grow with the number of busy workers (covers the concurrency part, which depends on load).
